- **jq: `path()` of a narrow literal slice no longer copies the document** (#6265).
  `path(.users[:10])` materialized the whole input to name one path component. A literal slice whose bounds alone keep it
  to 4096 elements or fewer (`[:10]`, `[10:20]`, `[-10:]`) now walks with cursors; a wider range, or one that depends on the
  array's length (`[1:]`, `[:-1]`), is unchanged, because reading it element by element costs more than the one copy.
  CPU time on a 36 MB file, min of 11 interleaved runs: 521 ms to 83 ms and 378 MB to 53 MB on an AMD Ryzen 9 7950X, 202 ms to
  56 ms and 316 MB to 49 MB on an Apple M4 Pro. A slice of a whole array that is most of the document gains less: 41 MB, 1102 ms
  to 412 ms and 386 ms to 338 ms. The one slower row is `path(.[:4096][].id)` on a 731 KB file on the M4 Pro, 7.7 ms to 8.8 ms
  (9.5 ms from 12.8 ms on the 7950X).
  Outputs are identical on every measured row and to jq 1.7.1 on well-formed documents. On a malformed one, a narrow slice now
  validates what `.a[s:e]` validates, so a fault outside the range no longer raises, as `path(.a[0])` already did; see the jq
  limitations page. A full-range literal slice validates through the cursor, which also rejects a nested `[,]`
  (`[.[0:3]] | length` on `[[,],2,3]`) that it used to answer, as jq rejects it.
